ANIMAL HEALTH KNOWLEDGE BASE — GENERAL REFERENCE
==================================================

This document contains general educational information about common animal health observations. It is intended for informational purposes only and does not replace professional veterinary advice.


REDUCED APPETITE / ANOREXIA
----------------------------

Reduced appetite in animals can indicate a wide range of conditions, from minor digestive discomfort to more serious illness.

In cattle (cows and buffaloes):
- Reduced feed intake lasting more than 24 hours should be monitored closely.
- Common causes include digestive issues (rumen acidosis, bloat, indigestion), infectious diseases (foot-and-mouth disease, hemorrhagic septicemia), metabolic conditions (ketosis in dairy cows, milk fever), or environmental stress (heat stress, sudden feed changes).
- Observe whether the animal is still drinking water, ruminating (chewing cud), and passing normal stool.
- In dairy cattle, reduced appetite often correlates with decreased milk production.

In goats:
- Goats are browsers and may reduce feed intake due to parasitic infections (particularly haemonchosis), respiratory infections, or dental problems.
- Internal parasites are one of the most common causes of appetite loss in goats.
- Check for pale mucous membranes (FAMACHA scoring), bottle jaw (submandibular edema), and rough coat.

In dogs:
- Dogs may skip meals occasionally without concern, but persistent appetite loss (more than 24–48 hours) warrants attention.
- Common causes include gastrointestinal issues, infections, dental disease, pain, or stress.
- Observe for other symptoms such as vomiting, diarrhea, lethargy, or behavioral changes.

In cats:
- Cats that stop eating for more than 24 hours are at risk of hepatic lipidosis (fatty liver disease), especially overweight cats.
- Appetite loss in cats should be taken seriously and evaluated promptly.
- Common causes include upper respiratory infections, dental disease, kidney disease, or stress.

In poultry:
- Reduced feed consumption in flocks may indicate infectious diseases (Newcastle disease, avian influenza, coccidiosis), heat stress, water quality issues, or mycotoxin contamination in feed.
- Monitor flock-level feed consumption and water intake together.


FEVER / ABNORMAL BODY TEMPERATURE
-----------------------------------

Normal body temperatures vary by species:
- Cattle: 38.0–39.5°C (100.4–103.1°F)
- Buffalo: 37.5–39.0°C (99.5–102.2°F)
- Goat: 38.5–40.0°C (101.3–104.0°F)
- Dog: 38.0–39.2°C (100.4–102.5°F)
- Cat: 38.0–39.2°C (100.4–102.5°F)
- Poultry: 40.5–42.0°C (104.9–107.6°F)

Fever indicates the body is fighting an infection or inflammation. Persistent fever (more than 24 hours) or very high fever requires veterinary attention.

In cattle: Fever combined with nasal discharge, coughing, or reduced milk yield may suggest respiratory infection (bovine respiratory disease) or systemic illness.

In dogs and cats: Fever with lethargy, vomiting, or diarrhea may suggest infection, tick-borne disease, or inflammatory conditions.


DIARRHEA
---------

Diarrhea in animals leads to dehydration and electrolyte loss and can become life-threatening, especially in young animals.

In calves:
- Neonatal calf diarrhea (scours) is a leading cause of calf mortality.
- Common causes include E. coli, rotavirus, coronavirus, cryptosporidium, and salmonella.
- Dehydration assessment: skin tent test (skin on neck or eyelid — if skin stays tented for more than 2 seconds, moderate to severe dehydration is likely).
- Oral rehydration therapy is critical for mild to moderate cases.

In goats:
- Diarrhea in goats may indicate coccidiosis (especially in kids), parasitic gastroenteritis, or enterotoxemia (overeating disease).
- Young goats (kids) are especially vulnerable.

In dogs:
- Acute diarrhea may result from dietary indiscretion, infections, or parasites.
- Bloody diarrhea (hemorrhagic gastroenteritis, parvovirus in unvaccinated puppies) requires urgent veterinary care.

In cats:
- Chronic diarrhea may indicate inflammatory bowel disease, food sensitivities, or parasites.
- Acute onset with vomiting may suggest infection or toxin ingestion.

In poultry:
- Bloody droppings may indicate coccidiosis.
- Watery or greenish droppings may indicate viral infections or salmonellosis.


VOMITING / REGURGITATION
--------------------------

Vomiting is common in dogs and cats. Cattle and goats do not truly vomit (regurgitation during rumination is normal).

In dogs:
- Occasional vomiting may not be concerning, but repeated vomiting, vomiting blood, or vomiting with lethargy requires veterinary evaluation.
- Common causes: dietary indiscretion, gastritis, foreign body ingestion, pancreatitis, or infections.

In cats:
- Frequent vomiting (more than once a week) is not normal and may indicate chronic kidney disease, hyperthyroidism, inflammatory bowel disease, or hairballs.
- Vomiting with inability to eat or drink requires prompt attention.

In ruminants (cattle, buffalo, goats):
- True vomiting is rare and potentially serious. Regurgitation during rumination is normal.
- If a ruminant appears to be vomiting, it may indicate severe bloat, poisoning, or obstruction.


COUGHING / RESPIRATORY DIFFICULTY
-----------------------------------

Respiratory symptoms should be evaluated based on severity, duration, and accompanying signs.

In cattle:
- Bovine Respiratory Disease (BRD) is a leading cause of illness and death in feedlot cattle and calves.
- Symptoms include coughing, nasal discharge, rapid breathing, fever, and depression.
- Stress (transport, overcrowding, weather changes) increases susceptibility.

In goats:
- Pneumonia is common in goats, especially during cold or wet weather.
- Caprine Arthritis Encephalitis (CAE) virus can cause chronic pneumonia in adult goats.

In dogs:
- Kennel cough (infectious tracheobronchitis) causes a dry, honking cough. Usually self-limiting but can be severe in puppies.
- Persistent cough may indicate heart disease, pneumonia, or collapsing trachea.

In cats:
- Feline upper respiratory infection (URI) is common and presents with sneezing, nasal discharge, and sometimes coughing.
- Asthma in cats presents with wheezing and labored breathing.

In poultry:
- Respiratory signs (gasping, sneezing, rattling) may indicate Newcastle disease, infectious bronchitis, avian influenza, or aspergillosis.
- Respiratory disease in poultry can spread rapidly through flocks.


DEHYDRATION
------------

Signs of dehydration across species:
- Dry or tacky mucous membranes (gums, nose)
- Sunken eyes
- Reduced skin elasticity (skin tent test)
- Decreased urination
- Weakness and lethargy

In cattle and buffalo: Skin tent test on the neck or upper eyelid. Tenting lasting more than 2 seconds suggests significant dehydration.

In dogs and cats: Check gum moisture and capillary refill time (press gum, color should return within 2 seconds).

Dehydration is dangerous in young animals and can escalate quickly. Oral or IV fluid therapy may be needed depending on severity.


LETHARGY / REDUCED ACTIVITY
------------------------------

Lethargy is a non-specific but important symptom. An animal that is less active, unresponsive, or reluctant to move may be experiencing pain, fever, metabolic imbalance, or systemic illness.

- In cattle: A cow that separates from the herd and stands with a hunched back may be in pain or seriously ill.
- In dogs: Sudden lethargy combined with other symptoms (vomiting, diarrhea, pale gums) requires prompt attention.
- In cats: Cats are naturally good at hiding illness. Any noticeable change in activity should be taken seriously.
- In poultry: Birds that are fluffed up, huddled, or separated from the flock may be ill.


SKIN ABNORMALITIES
--------------------

Skin conditions in animals may be caused by parasites (mange, ticks, fleas, lice), fungal infections (ringworm), bacterial infections, allergies, or nutritional deficiencies.

In cattle:
- Lumpy Skin Disease (LSD) presents as firm, raised nodules on the skin. It is a viral disease spread by insects.
- Mange (caused by mites) presents as hair loss, thickened skin, and intense itching.
- Tick infestations can transmit diseases like babesiosis and anaplasmosis.

In dogs:
- Common skin conditions include mange (demodectic or sarcoptic), flea allergy dermatitis, hot spots, and fungal infections.
- Hair loss patterns, itching intensity, and distribution help identify the cause.

In cats:
- Ringworm (dermatophytosis) is common and can be transmitted to humans (zoonotic).
- Flea allergy dermatitis causes intense scratching and hair loss, often near the tail base.

In goats:
- Mange and lice infestations are common, especially in crowded conditions.
- Orf (contagious ecthyma) causes scabby lesions around the mouth and is zoonotic.


GENERAL ANIMAL OBSERVATION GUIDELINES
----------------------------------------

When observing a potentially sick animal, note:

1. Behavior: Is the animal active, alert, and responsive? Or dull, depressed, and isolated?
2. Appetite and water intake: Has eating or drinking changed?
3. Body condition: Is the animal losing weight? Is the coat rough or dull?
4. Breathing: Is breathing normal, rapid, labored, or noisy?
5. Eyes and nose: Any discharge, swelling, or cloudiness?
6. Mouth and gums: Color (pale, yellow, blue indicates problems), moisture, lesions?
7. Stool and urine: Color, consistency, frequency, presence of blood or parasites?
8. Skin and coat: Hair loss, lesions, swelling, parasites, wounds?
9. Mobility: Is the animal walking normally? Any limping, stiffness, or reluctance to move?
10. Temperature: If possible, take rectal temperature and compare to normal range for the species.

Early observation and documentation of symptoms helps veterinarians make better assessments. When in doubt, seek professional veterinary evaluation promptly.
